HOW OUR PRICING WORKS

Every Wanderlyn price is a per-person price based on two people sharing a twin or double room. It is a land-only price: it begins when we meet you at the arrival point named in the itinerary and ends when we drop you at the departure point. Getting yourself to that arrival point is yours to arrange, and we explain why under "What the price does not include".

We publish three seasonal bands for every trip: green season, shoulder season and peak season. The band is set by the departure date, not by the booking date, so booking early does not move you into a cheaper band. Green season is genuinely cheaper because it is genuinely less comfortable in some way we will name explicitly in each trip entry. We do not use "green season" as a marketing word for "quiet".

Prices are modular. The number you see is the core journey, and the modules listed later in this document attach to it. Nothing in the core is removable: we do not sell a version of a trip with the guide taken out or two nights dropped, because the price of the core does not decompose that way and a half-trip is not a cheaper trip, it is a worse one. What you can do is add.

Prices are held for 21 days from the date we send you a written quote. After 21 days we requote, and the requote may be higher or lower. Once your deposit is received, your price is fixed for the life of the booking and no surcharge of any kind will be applied, including for currency movement or fuel. We have never applied a surcharge and the contract does not permit one.

Wanderlyn does not discount published prices, and an advisor cannot create a discount for you. There are exactly three reductions, they are published, and they can be combined with each other up to a maximum of nine percent off the core price. Group reduction: six or more travellers booked together on the same departure receive five percent off the core price for every member of the group. Compass Club reduction: travellers who have completed a previous Wanderlyn journey receive four percent off the core price, applied automatically, for life. Early booking reduction: a deposit received ten or more calendar months before departure earns two hundred dollars off per person. These three are the entire list. If you are offered anything else, it did not come from us.


TRIP ONE: KYOTO AND THE NAKASENDO WAY
Trip code JP-NAK. Japan. Nine days, eight nights.

Who it suits. This is the trip for someone who wants Japan at walking pace and has been put off by the coach-and-castle version of it. Half the journey is urban and slow: Kyoto, three nights, with mornings booked and afternoons free. The other half is the Nakasendo, the Edo-period highway between Kyoto and Tokyo, of which we walk the best-preserved section through the Kiso Valley over three days, staying in family-run inns in post towns that were built to house travellers doing exactly this four hundred years ago. It suits couples, solo travellers and people in their sixties and seventies who walk regularly. It does not suit anyone looking for a beach day or a nightlife trip.

When it runs and what it costs. JP-NAK departs twenty-two times a year.

Green season on JP-NAK is January to early March, and it costs $3,890 per person. It is cheaper because the Kiso Valley is cold, some of the higher trail sections may be closed by snow and substituted with a valley-floor alternative, and several of the smaller inns close for part of the winter, which means a night or two in a less characterful place. Kyoto in January is superb and almost empty. If the walking matters more to you than the emptiness, do not book green season.

Shoulder season on JP-NAK, the Kyoto and Nakasendo journey, is late March, June, September and November, and it costs $4,180 per person. This is the band most travellers book and the one we recommend. November is the single most requested departure of the year because of the maple colour in the Kiso Valley, and it sells out ten to twelve months ahead.

Peak season on JP-NAK, the Kyoto and Nakasendo journey, is the first two weeks of April and the last two weeks of October, and it costs $4,690 per person. April is cherry blossom, which is real, brief, and impossible to guarantee within a fixed departure date. We say clearly at the point of booking that an April departure is not a blossom guarantee. October is the most reliable weather of the year.

The single supplement on JP-NAK is $760. It buys a private room for all eight nights. Two of those nights are in traditional inns where a private room genuinely is a private room and not a partitioned one.

Itinerary, day by day.

Day one, Kyoto. Arrival, at your own pace, to our hotel in the Nakagyo district. Nothing is scheduled before six in the evening, when we hold a welcome dinner in a small kaiseki restaurant a five-minute walk from the hotel. Your guide is present from this dinner onward. If your flight lands late, you eat late, and we hold you a portion.

Day two, Kyoto. Eastern Kyoto on foot from eight in the morning, deliberately early: Kiyomizu-dera before the crowds arrive, then down through the stone lanes of Ninenzaka and Sannenzaka to Kodai-ji, ending at Yasaka Shrine before noon. The afternoon is free. Most people sleep.

Day three, Kyoto. Morning at Fushimi Inari, entering by the less-used southern gate, then a short train to Uji for green tea and the Byodo-in phoenix hall. Back in Kyoto by three. Free evening. Our guide will point you at three restaurants at three price levels and will book any of them for you.

Day four, Kyoto to Nakatsugawa to Magome. We leave Kyoto by rail after breakfast, change at Nagoya, and are in Magome by early afternoon. Magome is a restored post town on a steep cobbled slope with a view down the valley that is the reason people walk this route. Short orientation walk, then a first night in a family-run minshuku. Dinner is cooked by the family. There is no menu.

Day five, Magome to Tsumago. The signature walking day, and the easiest of the three: eight kilometres over the Magome Pass, roughly three and a half hours with stops, through cedar forest with two waterfalls and a bear bell you are expected to ring. Luggage goes ahead by van. Tsumago is the most completely preserved post town on the Nakasendo, and the local preservation covenant forbids visible cars, aerials and vending machines, which is why it looks the way it does.

Day six, Tsumago to Nojiri, then Kiso-Fukushima. A longer day, seventeen kilometres, five to six hours, following the old road and the river. This is the day that decides whether people describe the trip as a walking holiday or a cultural one. Night in Kiso-Fukushima, which was the checkpoint town where the shogunate inspected travellers, and the reconstructed barrier gate is worth the twenty minutes.

Day seven, Kiso-Fukushima and Narai. A rest day for the legs. Local train north to Narai, the wealthiest of the post towns and a kilometre long, with the best surviving merchant houses on the route. Free afternoon, and the local onsen is a four-minute walk from the inn. Our guides consider this the best day of the trip and it is the one people are most surprised by.

Day eight, Kiso-Fukushima to Tokyo. Rail to Tokyo, arriving early afternoon. Final night in Yanaka, a low-rise old-Tokyo neighbourhood deliberately chosen over Shinjuku or Shibuya, because arriving in Tokyo at speed after six days in the Kiso Valley is unpleasant and we would rather ease you in. Farewell dinner.

Day nine, Tokyo. Trip ends after breakfast. We hand you a printed sheet of directions to both Tokyo airports with times and costs. Late checkout to two in the afternoon is included.

What the JP-NAK price includes. Eight nights of accommodation: four in a three-star-equivalent Kyoto hotel, three in family-run inns on the Nakasendo, one in a small Tokyo hotel. All breakfasts. Three dinners, being the welcome dinner, both inn dinners on the Nakasendo, and the farewell dinner in Tokyo. Two lunches on the long walking days, packed by the inns. A Wanderlyn guide with you from day one dinner to day nine breakfast. All rail travel inside the itinerary, including the Kyoto to Nagoya to Nakatsugawa legs and the Kiso-Fukushima to Tokyo leg, in reserved second-class seats. All entry fees to every site named above. Luggage transfer by van on the three Nakasendo walking days, one bag per person up to twenty kilos. A local SIM or eSIM with ten gigabytes of data. Airport-to-hotel transfer on day one is not included and is available as a module.

What the JP-NAK price does not include, stated plainly. International flights to Japan and home again, which we do not sell and do not book, because we are not a licensed flight retailer and a flight bought through us would cost you more and give you fewer rights than one bought direct. Travel insurance, which is mandatory, see the insurance section. Visa costs, if your nationality requires one. Lunches on days one, two, three, four, seven, eight and nine. Dinners on days three, six and seven, which are deliberately free so you can eat where you like. All drinks, including at included meals, with the single exception of green tea and water at the inns. Tips and gratuities, see the tipping section. Onsen entry in Kiso-Fukushima, which is between four and eight dollars. Anything you buy. Any airport transfer at either end. Single supplement, if you want your own room. Any module.

How demanding it is. Moderate. Three walking days of eight, seventeen and zero kilometres, on well-made surfaces with some steep cobbled sections and one sustained climb to the Magome Pass of about three hundred metres. There is no scrambling, no exposure and no altitude. You need to be able to walk for five to six hours with breaks, on consecutive days, carrying a day pack. In Kyoto you will walk between eight and twelve kilometres a day on flat ground. Japanese inns involve sitting on the floor, sleeping on a futon on the floor, and getting back up again, which several travellers a year find harder than the walking. There are stairs everywhere in the post towns and no lifts.

Landmarks, and what is actually worth your time. Fushimi Inari is the ten thousand vermilion gates, and the entire experience is decided by when you go: the first four hundred metres are unpleasantly crowded by nine in the morning and almost empty at seven. We take you at seven. Kiyomizu-dera is a wooden stage on a hillside built without nails and the view back over Kyoto is the one on every poster; go, but go early. Byodo-in in Uji is on the ten-yen coin and takes forty minutes; the tea, at the shops along the river, takes longer and is the better memory. Tsumago is not a museum village, people live in those houses, and the correct way to see it is to be there in the evening after the day visitors leave, which is why we sleep there. Narai is the one nobody expects and the one people write to us about afterwards. The Magome Pass waterfalls are a five-minute detour and worth it.

Suggestions for your free time on JP-NAK. In Kyoto: the Nishiki market at opening rather than at lunch; the Philosopher's Path walked north to south in the late afternoon; Pontocho alley after dark, which is narrow, lantern-lit and entirely worth the twenty minutes it takes to walk; and the Kyoto International Manga Museum if it rains. In Kiso-Fukushima: the onsen, and the riverside path north of the barrier gate. In Tokyo: Yanaka Ginza street at dusk, the cemetery walk, and Ueno Park if you have a morning spare. Our guides carry a printed list of about forty of these and will write you a personal one on day two if you ask.


TRIP TWO: PATAGONIA, THE TORRES DEL PAINE TRAVERSE
Trip code PA-TDP. Chile and Argentina. Eleven days, ten nights.

Who it suits. The hardest trip we run, and the one we turn people away from most often. It is a genuine multi-day trek in a place with weather that can stop an itinerary dead. It suits experienced hill walkers who have done multi-day trips before, who own boots they have already broken in, and who understand that a day can be cancelled for wind. It does not suit first-time trekkers, and we will ask you directly about your last three walking trips before we take a deposit. We have declined bookings on this trip and we will do it again.

When it runs and what it costs. PA-TDP departs fourteen times a year and only in the southern summer.

Green season on PA-TDP is late October and all of March, and it costs $4,780 per person. It is cheaper because the weather is materially worse: more chance of snow on the passes in October, shorter days in March, and a higher chance that the John Gardner Pass day is substituted. Two of our fourteen departures a year are green season and they are booked almost entirely by people who have done the trip before.

Shoulder season on PA-TDP, the Torres del Paine traverse, is November and February, and it costs $5,340 per person. Long days, less wind than midsummer, and the best light for photography. This is the band we recommend and the one our guides book for themselves.

Peak season on PA-TDP, the Torres del Paine traverse, is December and January, and it costs $5,890 per person. It is the warmest and the busiest, the refugios are full, and the famous towers viewpoint at sunrise will have sixty people on it rather than fifteen. Daylight runs to almost eleven at night, which is the real argument for it.

The single supplement on PA-TDP is $940, and it is honest to say that it buys you less than on our other trips. Three of the ten nights are in mountain refugios where the accommodation is a bunk in a shared dormitory and a private room does not exist at any price. The supplement covers the other seven nights only, and it is priced accordingly.

Itinerary, day by day.

Day one, Punta Arenas to Puerto Natales. Meet at Punta Arenas airport at two in the afternoon, group transfer of three hours north to Puerto Natales. Gear check at six, which is not a formality: your guide will look at your boots, your waterproof shell and your layers, and will tell you what to rent. Welcome dinner.

Day two, Puerto Natales. A deliberate buffer day, and it exists because bags get lost. Half-day walk on the Sierra Dorotea ridge above town for the view over the Ultima Esperanza fjord, four hours, five hundred metres of ascent, which also lets your guide see how everyone actually moves. Afternoon free for last purchases. Final pack, with the guide, into what goes to the refugios and what stays behind.

Day three, into the park, Lake Grey. Transfer into Torres del Paine, catamaran across Lake Pehoe, and the first walking day: five hours north along the western shore to Refugio Grey with the Grey Glacier coming into view for the last hour. Eleven kilometres, gentle. First refugio night.

Day four, the glacier and the suspension bridges. A day walk north from Refugio Grey to the two suspension bridges over the glacier's edge, and if conditions allow, the third. Six hours out and back, fourteen kilometres. This is the day that most often gets shortened for wind, and shortening it is not a disappointment, it is the correct decision.

Day five, Grey to Paine Grande to Italiano. Back south along the lake to Paine Grande, then east into the French Valley, camping at Italiano. Eighteen kilometres, seven hours, the longest day so far and the one people find hardest, because the middle section is exposed and the wind on the Paine Grande flats is relentless.

Day six, the French Valley. Up into the French Valley to the Britanico viewpoint and back, eleven kilometres, five hours, with a thousand metres of hanging glacier above you on the left the entire way. The ice falls off it while you watch, several times an hour. Our guides consider this the finest day in the park and most of our travellers agree with them.

Day seven, Italiano to Los Cuernos to Chileno. East along the shore of Lake Nordenskjold under the Cuernos, then north into the Ascencio valley. Nineteen kilometres, eight hours, the longest day of the trip. Night at Refugio Chileno, positioned deliberately so that day eight is possible.

Day eight, the Towers at sunrise. Up at four in the morning for the final ninety-minute climb over the moraine to the Mirador Las Torres for sunrise on the three granite towers. It is a steep, loose, dark, cold hour and a half and it is the reason people come to Patagonia. Down for breakfast, then out of the valley to the hotel at Laguna Amarga. Fourteen kilometres in total. Hot shower, real bed.

Day nine, park day, and the crossing. A slow morning, the Salto Grande waterfall and the Nordenskjold viewpoint by vehicle, then the border crossing east into Argentina and on to El Calafate. Long driving day, about six hours including the border.

Day ten, Perito Moreno. Full day at the Perito Moreno glacier: the balcony walkways in the morning, and the boat approach to the northern face in the afternoon. The glacier calves constantly and audibly. Farewell dinner in El Calafate.

Day eleven, El Calafate. Trip ends after breakfast, with a group transfer to El Calafate airport for any flight after eleven in the morning.

What the PA-TDP price includes. Ten nights: two in Puerto Natales, three in mountain refugios in shared dormitories, one at Refugio Chileno, one at a hotel at Laguna Amarga, and three in El Calafate. All breakfasts. All dinners on days three through eight, being the refugio and trek days, because there is nowhere else to eat. Packed trail lunches on every walking day. The welcome and farewell dinners. A Wanderlyn mountain guide and a second assistant guide throughout, which is a ratio we do not compromise on this trip. All park entry fees, currently ninety dollars, and the catamaran across Lake Pehoe. All ground transport including the Chile to Argentina border crossing. The Perito Moreno boat approach. Duffel transfer to the Laguna Amarga hotel. Emergency satellite communicator carried by the guides.

What the PA-TDP price does not include, stated plainly. International and domestic flights, including the Santiago to Punta Arenas leg, which almost everyone needs and which we will tell you how to book but will not book for you. Travel insurance including the mandatory evacuation cover, see the insurance section. The Chile reciprocity or tourist fee if your nationality is charged one. Argentina entry costs. Lunches on days one, two, nine, ten and eleven. Dinners on days two, nine is included, so dinners on day two only. All alcohol, including at included meals. Trekking equipment: boots, poles, shell, sleeping bag. All of it is rentable in Puerto Natales and there is a module for it. Hot showers at two of the three refugios, which are metered and cost about five dollars. Tips. Single supplement. Any module.

How demanding it is. Hard, and this is the section to read twice. Six consecutive walking days of eleven, fourteen, eighteen, eleven, nineteen and fourteen kilometres, with cumulative ascent over the six days of about four thousand two hundred metres. The longest day is eight hours moving, not eight hours elapsed. Three nights are in dormitory bunks with shared bathrooms. The sunrise climb on day eight is done in the dark on loose moraine with a head torch. There is no altitude problem, the highest point is under a thousand metres, and there is no technical ground, no rope and no ice axe. What there is, is wind: sustained winds of eighty kilometres an hour are normal, gusts above a hundred and twenty happen, and the guides will turn a day around for it. You must be able to walk eight hours with a seven-kilo day pack, on consecutive days, in cold rain, and be reasonable company at the end of it.

Landmarks, and what is actually worth your time. The Mirador Las Torres at sunrise is the photograph, and it deserves its reputation, but be honest with yourself that you are looking at it for twenty minutes after ninety minutes of climbing in the dark, and roughly one departure in four does not get the light. The Britanico viewpoint in the French Valley is, in our guides' opinion, the better day and almost nobody photographs it. Grey Glacier from the suspension bridges is the most physically startling thing on the trip. Perito Moreno is the only landmark on this itinerary you can reach without walking, and it is the one that reduces people to silence. The Cuernos from the Nordenskjold shore on day seven is the classic Patagonia postcard and you walk under it for four hours.

Suggestions for your free time on PA-TDP. There is less free time on this trip than on any other we run, by design. In Puerto Natales on day two: the Municipal History Museum is small and good on the Kaweskar people, the waterfront at sunset is the best hour of the day, and there are two breweries. At the refugios in the evening: the correct thing to do is sit outside with a beer until it gets cold, which in December is around eleven at night. In El Calafate on day nine and ten evenings: the Glaciarium ice museum is genuinely worth two hours, and the lakeside bird reserve at Laguna Nimez has flamingos and takes ninety minutes.


TRIP THREE: MOROCCO, THE ATLAS, THE SAHARA AND THE MEDINAS
Trip code MA-ASM. Morocco. Eight days, seven nights.

Who it suits. Our most popular trip and the one we most often recommend to people travelling with older children or to first-time travellers to North Africa. It moves between three completely different Moroccos in a week: Marrakech, the High Atlas villages, and the Erg Chebbi dunes. The walking is optional and moderate. It suits families with children aged eight and over, mixed-ability groups, and anyone who wants a lot of variety in a short time. It does not suit someone looking for a single-base relaxing week.

When it runs and what it costs. MA-ASM departs thirty-one times a year, more than any other Wanderlyn trip.

Green season on MA-ASM is July and August, and it costs $2,410 per person. It is cheaper because it is genuinely, seriously hot: forty-five degrees Celsius in Marrakech and in the desert is normal, the Atlas walking is moved to before eight in the morning, and the afternoons become rest time whether you wanted them to be or not. We say this plainly and about a third of people who ask about summer choose a different month once we have.

Shoulder season on MA-ASM, the Morocco journey, is April, May, September and October, and it costs $2,860 per person. This is the band to book. Warm days, cold desert nights, and the Atlas villages at their best.

Peak season on MA-ASM, the Morocco journey, is late December and the first week of January, and it costs $3,240 per person. Cool, clear, busy, and the desert nights are cold enough that the camp uses proper bedding and a fire.

The single supplement on MA-ASM is $520, which is the lowest of our five trips, and it covers all seven nights including a private tent at the desert camp.

Itinerary, day by day.

Day one, Marrakech. Arrival at your own pace to our riad in the Kasbah quarter of the medina. Nothing scheduled until five in the afternoon, when we walk to the Jemaa el-Fnaa together as it changes over from a day market to a night one, which takes about forty minutes and is the best free thing in the city. Welcome dinner on a roof terrace.

Day two, Marrakech. Guided morning through the medina: the Ben Youssef Madrasa, the Saadian Tombs, and the souks with an explanation of how the trades are grouped, which is the thing that makes the souks navigable rather than overwhelming. Back at the riad by one. Afternoon free. Optional cooking module runs this afternoon.

Day three, Marrakech to the High Atlas. Drive south over the Tizi n'Tichka, stopping at Ait Ben Haddou, the fortified earthen village on the old caravan route that you have seen in a dozen films. Two hours there including the climb to the granary at the top. On to Skoura and a night in a kasbah among the palm groves.

Day four, the Dades and the Todra. Up the Dades Gorge in the morning, then east to the Todra Gorge where a three-hundred-metre limestone wall narrows to ten metres wide, and a flat, easy walk of about an hour up the riverbed. Afternoon drive to Merzouga at the edge of the Erg Chebbi.

Day five, the Sahara. The day the trip is sold on. Camel trek or four-wheel-drive into the dunes in the late afternoon, ninety minutes, arriving at our permanent camp for sunset from the top of the highest dune. Dinner around a fire, Gnawa drumming from the camp staff, and a sky with no light pollution in any direction. Beds are in proper canvas tents with real mattresses and a shared bathroom block with hot water.

Day six, Merzouga to the Draa Valley. Sunrise over the dunes, which is a fifteen-minute walk from your tent and the reason to go to bed early. Out of the desert and west through the Draa Valley and its palmeries, with a stop at a nomadic family's tent where we are expected and welcome. Night in Nkob or Agdz depending on the departure.

Day seven, back over the Atlas to Marrakech. The long return drive, about six hours with stops, arriving mid-afternoon. Free time in the medina. Farewell dinner.

Day eight, Marrakech. Trip ends after breakfast. Late checkout to noon included, and the riad will hold your bags all day at no charge.

What the MA-ASM price includes. Seven nights: three in a Marrakech riad, one in a kasbah at Skoura, one in a hotel at Merzouga, one in a permanent desert camp, one in the Draa Valley. All breakfasts. All dinners except day two and day seven's free evening options, so five dinners including the welcome dinner, the desert camp dinner and the farewell dinner. Three lunches on the long driving days. A Wanderlyn guide throughout and a separate professional driver, which is not the same person, deliberately. All ground transport in a private air-conditioned minibus. The camel trek or the four-wheel-drive into the dunes, your choice, made on the day. All entry fees including Ait Ben Haddou, the Ben Youssef Madrasa and the Saadian Tombs. Bottled water throughout the trip, unlimited, which in July is not a small line item. The Gnawa musicians at the camp.

What the MA-ASM price does not include, stated plainly. International flights to Marrakech. Travel insurance, mandatory. Lunches on days one, two, five and eight. Dinner on day two, which is deliberately free. All drinks other than the bottled water, including at included meals. Alcohol, which is available at the riad and the hotels but not at the desert camp and not in the Atlas villages. Tips for the guide, the driver and the camp staff. Hammam and spa treatments at the riad. Anything bought in the souks, and no, our guide does not take a commission from any shop, which is why we do not take you to a carpet warehouse. Single supplement. Any module.

How demanding it is. Easy to moderate, and the least demanding trip we run. The only committed walking is the hour in the Todra Gorge on flat gravel, the climb to the granary at Ait Ben Haddou which is twenty minutes of steps, and the fifteen-minute dune walk for sunrise, which is in soft sand and is harder than it sounds. Everything else is optional. What is genuinely demanding is the driving: three days involve four to six hours in a vehicle on mountain roads with switchbacks, and if you are prone to motion sickness this is the trip detail to take seriously. Heat is the other factor, and in green season it is the dominant one. The camel trek is ninety minutes on a camel, which most people find fun for forty minutes and endurable for ninety, and you can walk or take the vehicle instead at no cost and with no fuss.

Landmarks, and what is actually worth your time. Jemaa el-Fnaa is at its best in the ninety minutes around sunset and is a tourist trap at ten in the morning. Ait Ben Haddou is a UNESCO site and deserves it, but the climb to the granary at the top is what makes it, and about half of visitors do not do it. The Todra Gorge is best in the two hours after opening before the tour buses arrive from Ouarzazate, which is why we go early. The Erg Chebbi dunes at sunrise, from the top of the dune behind camp, is the single image people take home from this trip. The Ben Youssef Madrasa reopened after a long restoration and the courtyard is the finest piece of Marinid craftsmanship in the country. The Saadian Tombs take twenty-five minutes and have a queue; go, but do not build a morning around it.

Suggestions for your free time on MA-ASM. In Marrakech: the Jardin Majorelle needs a timed ticket bought in advance and our guide will do it for you if you ask on day one; Le Jardin Secret in the medina is quieter and half the price; the tanneries in the north of the medina are extraordinary and smell it, and you should go with someone rather than accepting a stranger's offer at the entrance; and a hammam is the correct use of a hot afternoon. At Skoura: walk into the palm grove at dusk. At Merzouga: the fossil workshops in the village are a genuine local industry and not a tourist invention. On the last afternoon: the rooftop of the Cafe des Epices for a mint tea and the medina from above.


TRIP FOUR: THE AMALFI COAST AND CILENTO SLOW FOOD JOURNEY
Trip code IT-ACS. Italy. Seven days, six nights.

Who it suits. A food and wine journey with enough walking to justify the eating. It pairs the Amalfi Coast, which everyone has heard of, with the Cilento, immediately south of it, which almost nobody has, and which is where the Mediterranean diet was first documented. It suits couples, food-interested travellers, groups of friends, and people who want a European trip that is not a city break. It does not suit anyone on a strict budget, because Italy in season is expensive and we do not pretend otherwise.

When it runs and what it costs. IT-ACS departs eighteen times a year.

Green season on IT-ACS is November and March, and it costs $3,010 per person. It is cheaper because the coast is genuinely closed in parts: the Path of the Gods is walkable but some coastal restaurants and the smaller ferries are not running, and the sea is too cold to swim. It is also, for the same reasons, the only time you will have Positano to yourself.

Shoulder season on IT-ACS, the Amalfi Coast and Cilento journey, is April, May, early June, late September and October, and it costs $3,420 per person. October is shoulder season on IT-ACS for the whole month; there is no late-October peak band on this trip. The band we recommend. Everything is open, the walking is comfortable, and you can swim from late May.

Peak season on IT-ACS, the Amalfi Coast and Cilento journey, is late June through August, and it costs $3,980 per person. Hot, crowded, expensive locally, and the coast road is slow. Our guides do not recommend it and we say so, but the sea is warm and the evenings are long.

The single supplement on IT-ACS is $680.

Itinerary, day by day.

Day one, Sorrento. Arrival at your own pace to our hotel above the Marina Grande. Welcome dinner at seven with a Campanian wine flight, four glasses, explained.

Day two, the Path of the Gods. Bus to Bomerano and the Sentiero degli Dei, the seven-kilometre cliff path from Bomerano to Nocelle, four hundred metres above the sea for the whole of it, three hours with stops. Down the fifteen hundred steps to Positano, or the bus for anyone who would rather not. Afternoon and evening free in Positano, ferry back to Sorrento at six or eight.

Day three, Amalfi and Ravello. Ferry along the coast to Amalfi, the cathedral and the cloister, then up to Ravello for the Villa Rufolo gardens and the terrace that Wagner wrote about. Long lunch at a family agriturismo in the hills above Scala, five courses, entirely from their own land. Back by late afternoon.

Day four, Sorrento to the Cilento. South past Salerno and into the Cilento National Park. On to our agriturismo near Castellabate, our base for three nights, which makes its own mozzarella di bufala at six every morning.

Day five, the Cilento. Morning at the mozzarella dairy for the six o'clock make, for anyone who wants to get up, which is optional and about half do. Late morning walk on the coastal path at Punta Licosa, five kilometres, easy, with a swimming stop. Afternoon free. Evening cooking session at the agriturismo, hands-on, four dishes, and you eat what you make.

Day six, Paestum and the Vallo di Diano. Buffalo farm and a fig and olive producer in the morning, both working farms and neither a visitor centre. How demanding it is. Moderate on one day and easy on the rest. The Path of the Gods is seven kilometres of well-marked but genuinely exposed cliff path with uneven stone underfoot, some narrow sections with a long drop, and no shade for most of it. Anyone seriously uncomfortable with heights should skip it and we will get you to Positano by road, at no extra cost, and nobody will mention it again. The fifteen hundred steps down to Positano are optional. The Punta Licosa walk is five easy kilometres. Everything else is a vehicle, a ferry and a table. The Amalfi Coast has stairs everywhere and no lifts; Positano in particular is a staircase with a town attached.

Landmarks, and what is actually worth your time. Paestum is the most underrated major site in Italy and the Temple of Neptune is better preserved than anything in Athens; give it two hours and go into the small museum for the Tomb of the Diver. Villa Rufolo's terrace in Ravello is the view, and it is best in the hour before closing when the day trippers have gone. The Amalfi cathedral cloister is small, Arab-Norman, and takes fifteen minutes that people skip. The Certosa di Padula is enormous, almost empty, and slightly eerie, and about a third of our travellers name it as their favourite thing on the trip, which surprises us every time.

Suggestions for your free time on IT-ACS. TRIP FIVE: KERALA, THE BACKWATERS AND THE WESTERN GHATS
Trip code IN-KWG. India. Ten days, nine nights.

Who it suits. The gentlest of our long trips and the one we recommend for a first trip to India. It runs from the tea and cardamom hills of the Western Ghats down through a wildlife reserve to the backwaters and the Arabian Sea, and the pace is deliberately slow: no day has more than three hours of driving. It suits couples, solo travellers, families with children aged eight and over, and travellers in their seventies. It does not suit someone wanting the north India circuit of forts and palaces, which is a different trip that we do not run.

When it runs and what it costs. IN-KWG departs sixteen times a year and never in the monsoon.

Green season on IN-KWG is June and September, and it costs $2,180 per person. It is cheaper because it brackets the monsoon: June is the beginning of the southwest monsoon and September the tail of it, the hills are spectacular and green, and it will rain hard for part of most days. Some walking is cancelled. Leeches are a genuine feature of the Ghats in these months and we provide salt and socks.

Shoulder season on IN-KWG, the Kerala journey, is February, March and late October, and it costs $2,540 per person. Warm, dry, and the band we recommend.

Peak season on IN-KWG, the Kerala journey, is late December and January, and it costs $2,890 per person. The best weather of the year, the busiest, and the only time the hill stations get genuinely cold at night.

The single supplement on IN-KWG is $610, and it covers eight of the nine nights. The houseboat night on the backwaters is a two-cabin boat and a solo traveller has a cabin to themselves at no supplement, which is the one place in our pricing where being alone is free.

Itinerary, day by day.

Day one, Kochi. Arrival at your own pace to a heritage homestay in Fort Kochi. Evening walk to the Chinese fishing nets for sunset, then a Kathakali performance with the pre-show explanation of the makeup and the hand gestures, which is what makes it comprehensible. Welcome dinner.

Day two, Kochi. Morning walking tour of Fort Kochi and Mattancherry: the Dutch Palace, the Paradesi Synagogue, the spice warehouses on Jew Town road where the smell in the street is cardamom and ginger. Afternoon free. Optional cooking module runs this afternoon.

Day three, Kochi to Munnar. Drive east and up into the Western Ghats, three hours, climbing to sixteen hundred metres through tea. Afternoon at a working tea estate with the plucking and the withering and the tasting, done by the estate's own people rather than a demonstration. Night in a tea bungalow.

Day four, Munnar. Sunrise from the Top Station viewpoint above the Nilgiri hills. Morning walk through the tea, eight kilometres, easy gradients, four hours. Afternoon free at the bungalow or the optional spice garden visit at Chinnakanal.

Day five, Munnar to Thekkady. South to the Periyar Tiger Reserve, three hours. Afternoon boat on Periyar Lake, which is the reliable way to see elephant, gaur, sambar and about ninety bird species, and the unreliable but real way to see a tiger. Night in Thekkady.

Day six, Periyar. Early morning guided walk inside the reserve with a forest department tracker, three hours, which is the part of the trip our wildlife-interested travellers care most about. Afternoon at a cardamom and pepper plantation, and the Kalaripayattu martial arts demonstration in the evening, which is loud and genuinely impressive.

Day seven, Thekkady to Alleppey and the houseboat. West and down out of the hills to the backwaters, four hours, the longest drive of the trip. Board a private houseboat in the early afternoon, and the boat moves through the canals and the paddy fields until sunset, moors overnight, and the crew of three cook for you on board. This is the night people book the trip for.

Day eight, the backwaters to Marari. Off the boat after breakfast, short transfer to Marari beach on the Arabian Sea. Nothing scheduled. A beach hut, a hammock and the sea.

Day nine, Marari. Also nothing scheduled, deliberately, and this is the only day on any Wanderlyn trip with no plan at all. Optional Ayurveda module runs today and tomorrow morning. Farewell dinner on the beach.

Day ten, departure. Trip ends after breakfast, with a group transfer to Kochi airport, ninety minutes, for any flight after one in the afternoon.

What the IN-KWG price includes. Nine nights: two in a Fort Kochi homestay, two in a Munnar tea bungalow, two in Thekkady, one on a private houseboat, two in beach huts at Marari. All breakfasts. All dinners, all nine of them, because eating where you are staying is how this trip works. Five lunches, including everything on the houseboat. A Wanderlyn guide throughout and a separate professional driver. All ground transport in a private air-conditioned vehicle. The Periyar boat and, separately, the guided reserve walk with the forest tracker, which requires a permit we obtain in advance. The tea estate visit, the plantation visit, the Kathakali performance and the Kalaripayattu demonstration. All entry and permit fees. Bottled or filtered water throughout.

What the IN-KWG price does not include, stated plainly. International flights to Kochi. Travel insurance, mandatory. The India e-visa, which almost every nationality needs and which costs between twenty-five and eighty dollars depending on your passport, applied for online at least four days before travel. Lunches on days one, two, four, nine and ten. Alcohol anywhere, and note that Kerala's licensing means it is not available at every property and is not available at all on the houseboat. Drinks other than water and the tea and coffee included at meals. Tips for the guide, the driver, the houseboat crew and the tracker. Ayurveda treatments, which are a module. Camera fees at certain sites, which are small and local. Single supplement. Any module.

How demanding it is. Easy to moderate. The only real walking is the eight-kilometre tea estate walk on day four, on paths and estate roads with gentle gradients, and the three-hour reserve walk on day six, which is flat but is on uneven ground and involves crossing a stream on a bamboo raft. Nothing else is more than an hour on foot. There is no altitude issue at sixteen hundred metres. The heat and humidity are the real load, particularly on the coast, and travellers who struggle on this trip almost always struggle with humidity rather than distance. The houseboat has a step down into the cabin and no rail. Roads in the Ghats have switchbacks and motion sickness is worth planning for.

Landmarks, and what is actually worth your time. The Chinese fishing nets at Fort Kochi are the postcard and they are best in the twenty minutes after sunset when the light goes orange and the nets are silhouettes. The Paradesi Synagogue is the oldest active synagogue in the Commonwealth and takes half an hour, and the hand-painted Cantonese floor tiles, no two the same, are the detail worth looking for. Top Station at sunrise is worth the four-thirty alarm about three mornings in four; on the fourth you are inside a cloud. The Periyar reserve walk is the best wildlife hour of the trip and the boat is the more reliable one, and they are different experiences rather than substitutes. The backwater canals in the two hours before sunset from the deck of a moving houseboat are, according to our own guides, the single most beautiful thing on any of our five trips.

Suggestions for your free time on IN-KWG. In Fort Kochi: the Kashi Art Cafe for breakfast; the Dutch cemetery; the street art along Bazaar Road; and Fort Kochi beach at dusk when the whole town walks. In Munnar: the tea museum is better than it sounds and the Lockhart estate road at dawn has almost no traffic. In Thekkady: the spice shops in the village genuinely sell better cardamom than anywhere at home, at a quarter of the price. At Marari: walk south along the sand at six in the morning when the fishing boats come in and the catch is auctioned on the beach, which is not a performance for visitors and is the best hour of the trip.

Solo travellers are welcome and are about a third of everyone who travels with us. You can share a twin room with another solo traveller of the same gender at no supplement, and we will tell you before departure whether we found you a match; if we did not, you get the single room and you do not pay the supplement. That is our risk, not yours. Or you can pay the single supplement and have your own room from the start.

Accessibility, answered honestly. None of our five trips is wheelchair accessible, and we will not tell you otherwise to make a sale. IT-ACS and MA-ASM have the least walking and IT-ACS has the most stairs. IN-KWG is the most manageable trip for someone with limited mobility, with the important exceptions of the step down into the houseboat cabin and the uneven ground on the Periyar reserve walk, which can be skipped. If you have a mobility, sensory or cognitive access need, call us and describe it, and we will give you a straight answer about which trips work and which do not, including "none of them", which we have said before.

Dietary requirements are handled on every trip and we need them at the time of booking, not later. Vegetarian is straightforward everywhere. Vegan is straightforward on IN-KWG and IT-ACS, workable on MA-ASM, and harder on JP-NAK, where dashi stock is in almost everything and we will arrange it but the choice at some inns will be narrow. Gluten-free is straightforward on IN-KWG and JP-NAK, and harder on IT-ACS, for reasons that are obvious. Serious allergies must be declared in writing at booking, we will confirm in writing what each property has agreed to, and on PA-TDP a severe nut allergy needs a conversation because the refugios cook for everyone at once.


ON THE TRIP: WHAT A DAY IS LIKE

A typical day starts with breakfast between seven and eight, has one guided element that runs three to five hours, and leaves the rest of the day to you. We schedule less than most operators do, on purpose. Our feedback consistently says the free afternoons are the most valued thing on the trip and the thing people would not give up.

Your guide is with you from the welcome dinner on day one to breakfast on the final day. They eat with the group at included meals, they are contactable at all hours, and they will book restaurants, arrange taxis, translate, and tell you which of the three things you are choosing between is actually worth it. They are not a walking encyclopaedia, and a good guide's most useful sentence is "I do not know, let me find out."

Group sizes being what they are, we will not put you in a coach with a microphone. Ground transport is a private minibus or a car, and on JP-NAK and IT-ACS it is largely public rail and ferry, which is faster and better.

Luggage. One main bag up to twenty kilos plus a day pack, on every trip. PA-TDP requires a soft duffel rather than a wheeled case because of the refugio transfers, and JP-NAK is easier with a case because of the station platforms. Anything over the allowance on the transfer days is a module or is left behind.

Connectivity. Japan and Italy have excellent coverage everywhere on the itinerary. Morocco has good coverage except in the deep desert, and the camp has no signal, which we regard as a feature. Kerala has good coverage except in parts of the Periyar reserve. Patagonia has no coverage inside the park for four of the six trekking days, and the guides carry the satellite communicator for emergencies, which is not a phone.

Tipping. It is not included on any trip and it is genuinely optional. For guidance, because people ask and because the ambiguity is uncomfortable: on a one-week trip our travellers commonly leave the guide between eighty and a hundred and twenty dollars per person for the trip, the driver between thirty and fifty, and the houseboat or camp crew about twenty. On PA-TDP the two guides are usually given a hundred and fifty each. There is no tipping culture in Japan and tipping in restaurants there is not appreciated; tip your Wanderlyn guide if you wish to, and nobody else.

What to bring is on a per-trip packing list sent with your final documents twenty-one days out. The short version: broken-in boots on PA-TDP and JP-NAK, a serious waterproof shell on PA-TDP and IN-KWG in green season, a hat and a factor fifty on MA-ASM, one warm layer even in the Sahara because the desert at night in January is close to freezing, and shoulders and knees covered for temple and mosque visits on JP-NAK, MA-ASM and IN-KWG.


WEATHER, MONTH BY MONTH, IN ONE PARAGRAPH EACH

Japan on JP-NAK. January and February are cold, two to nine degrees, dry and clear in Kyoto and snowy in the Kiso Valley. March warms quickly. April is fifteen to twenty and is the blossom lottery. June is the rainy season and is genuinely wet. September is warm, humid and carries a typhoon risk that has caused us to reroute a rail day twice in fourteen years. October and November are the best months, fifteen to twenty-two, dry and clear, with the maple colour in mid to late November.

Patagonia on PA-TDP. There is no month without wind. October is five to twelve degrees with snow possible on the passes. November and December are eight to seventeen with the longest daylight, up to seventeen hours. January is the warmest at ten to nineteen and the windiest, with sustained winds over eighty kilometres an hour on many days. February is similar and slightly calmer. March is the most stable weather of the season with the shortest days. Rain is possible on any day in any month and four seasons in a day is not a figure of speech here.

Morocco on MA-ASM. December and January are twelve to nineteen in Marrakech, cold at night in the Atlas and near freezing in the desert after dark. March to May is eighteen to twenty-eight and is close to perfect. June through August is thirty-eight to forty-five in Marrakech and hotter in the desert, and this is what the green season price reflects. September and October return to twenty-two to thirty. Rain is rare and brief in any month.

Italy on IT-ACS. November and March are ten to sixteen with rain likely and the sea too cold. April and May are sixteen to twenty-three, dry and green. June to August is twenty-eight to thirty-four with a warm sea and a crowded coast road. September and October are twenty to twenty-six and are, in our guides' opinion, the best weeks of the year on the Amalfi Coast.

Kerala on IN-KWG. December and January are twenty-two to thirty-two on the coast, dry, and pleasantly cool at night in Munnar at twelve to fourteen. February and March warm up to thirty-four on the coast and stay comfortable in the hills. June and September sit inside the southwest monsoon with heavy rain most days, spectacular hills and a genuine leech presence in the Ghats. April and May are hot and humid and we do not run departures then. October and November are the tail of the second monsoon and can go either way.
